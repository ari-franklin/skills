# Argument Depth

Use this when a post idea is plausible but risks becoming clean, short, and forgettable.

## Depth Test

A Superblog post is deep enough when the reader can see:

- what real pressure made the lesson matter
- why the old belief used to be reasonable
- what changed in the environment or system
- what did not change despite the new framing
- what a smart person might argue instead
- what decision or behavior should change
- what second-order consequence appears after the obvious takeaway

If the post cannot answer these, strengthen the argument before drafting.

## Common Thin Draft Pattern

Thin drafts often have:

- a true but familiar thesis
- clean section headings
- examples that illustrate but do not prove
- no serious counterargument
- no mechanism explaining why the pattern happens
- a practical takeaway that is really just a mindset shift
- revision notes asking the user to add the missing substance later

Do not return that draft. Add the missing substance, or ask for the real story if it cannot be inferred.

## Required Moves

When strengthening an idea, identify:

- `Scene`: the moment, pressure, or observed pattern that grounds the post
- `Old Rule`: the belief or practice the reader already understands
- `Why It Worked`: the conditions that made the old rule useful
- `Mechanism`: what changed, broke, or became visible
- `Competing View`: what a thoughtful reader might object to
- `Implication`: what should change in a decision, habit, or tradeoff
- `Limit`: where the new idea can become wrong or overused
- `Ending Turn`: the sharpened final thought that makes the post feel earned

## Evidence Standards

Use the strongest available evidence:

- real personal or workplace experience from the user
- observed pattern stated as observed, not invented as personal memory
- plausible hypothetical labeled as hypothetical
- product or team mechanism explained clearly enough to be judged

Never invent personal experience. If the story matters and is missing, ask for it.

## Second-Order Implications

Look for consequences beyond the obvious:

- What gets easier but more dangerous?
- What signal used to mean something but now means less?
- What constraint used to enforce discipline but now must be chosen intentionally?
- What does the new advice make people overcorrect toward?
- What becomes scarce when the old bottleneck gets cheaper?

The second-order implication is often the real post.

## Fail Conditions

Fail and revise before returning when:

- the post could be summarized as a single tidy heuristic without losing much
- the argument would fit better as a short social post
- the examples are interchangeable with any article on the topic
- the conclusion only says "be thoughtful," "communicate better," or "use judgment"
- the draft sounds like it was generated from the title alone
