# Image Style

Superblog images should feel like editorial companions to the argument, not generic blog decoration.

## Purpose

Use images to make a product or leadership tension easier to feel:

- the tradeoff in the post
- the old belief under pressure
- the constraint that changed
- the decision the reader needs to make
- the consequence of getting the idea wrong

Do not create images before the argument is clear. A weak thesis produces vague visuals.

## Visual Taste

Prefer:

- grounded workplace, product, or maker scenes with a specific tension
- concrete metaphors that explain a choice, constraint, or system behavior
- editorial still-life compositions using recognizable objects
- quiet, thoughtful lighting and restrained color
- one clear focal point with room for headline/crop flexibility

Avoid:

- generic people staring at laptops
- abstract gradients, glowing orbs, bokeh, or vague tech atmosphere
- stock-photo handshakes, conference rooms, dashboards, and whiteboards unless the scene has a precise narrative job
- literal robot imagery unless the post is directly about AI embodiment
- text-heavy images, fake UI text, unreadable sticky notes, or quote posters
- overly dramatic metaphors that make the post feel less practical

## Composition Rules

- Design for a horizontal hero crop first unless the user asks otherwise.
- Keep the main subject readable at thumbnail size.
- Leave negative space for optional title treatment, but do not put title text in the image by default.
- Use 1-3 meaningful objects or figures, not clutter.
- Make the image specific enough that it could not fit any generic productivity article.

## Prompt Standards

Every image prompt should include:

- medium or style
- subject and scene
- metaphor or argument role
- composition and camera/framing
- lighting and color direction
- constraints on text, logos, and generic stock-photo signals

Use natural-language prompts that an image model can follow directly. Do not include internal reasoning or a list of abstract themes without visual translation.

## Good Directions

- A half-built prototype sitting beside a worn notebook full of repeated problem observations.
- A roadmap board shown as movable reservation cards, with one card deliberately held aside.
- A small team choosing between a polished demo on a screen and a messy evidence board beside it.
- A toolbench where a single useful tool is worn from repeated use while shiny unused tools remain untouched.

## Bad Directions

- A futuristic AI dashboard.
- A product manager working on a laptop.
- Abstract network connections.
- A person looking thoughtful in an office.
- A generic maze, mountain, rocket, chessboard, or lightbulb.
